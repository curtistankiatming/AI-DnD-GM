The larger weakness is that the plot advances more fully than the relationships: I know what the party accomplished, but have heard too little from the people involved for the bell's rescue to feel like saving a home.

## Findings, most important first

### 1. Sable is defeated before he can make his case

**Where:** `bell-vault`, reached through `vault-antechamber` / `player-plan`; `expose-sable`; then `ending` / `report-bell`.

**What:** I chose “Enter together and confront Sable with what you know.” The next scene describes him standing with a chain, then offers the ledger or force. A successful ledger check immediately says: “His followers cut the binding, disarm him and surrender him to your party.” Sable never explains himself on this route. The ending then says only, “The immediate threat beneath Briarwatch has ended.” There is no exchange with the freed spirit or Tovin before payment. Supplementary reading confirmed that the vault opening contains no statement of Sable's reasons.

**Why it matters:** The bible's central question—who gets to decide what protects people—cannot become a difficult choice if the first antagonist never argues for his answer.

**Suggestion:** Keep the peaceful victory, but precede it with a short confrontation: Sable names the danger he believes only his binding can stop; Maren asks who consented; the player can question his evidence or present the ledger. Follow surrender with a brief response from the freed spirit and a homecoming beat with Tovin. Replace “Evidence wins what a blade need not” with a concrete reaction, such as a follower lowering their weapon after recognising a payment in the ledger. These additions need no extra check or reward.

**Severity:** major.

### 2. The companions' plans are menu labels rather than a conversation

**Where:** `vault-antechamber`, especially `player-plan`; `town-briarwatch`, the Orin and Maren personal-story actions; `town-ashford`, recruitment.

**What:** “Your companions speak in low voices; each has a different answer” leads straight to choices labelled “Follow Maren's plan” and “Follow Orin's plan.” We never hear them put those plans to each other. The optional town lines are better: Orin says, “We left civilians behind once. Not again.” Maren says, “Even if we cannot save them, their families deserve the truth.” Nessa's eventual introduction is just “Nessa Reed joins through the roadwardens’ guild,” followed by equipment and level information.

**Why it matters:** The bible gives these companions distinct voices and relationships, but the played route mostly reports their functions rather than letting those personalities emerge.

**Suggestion:** Put a short exchange before the vault choices: Orin identifies what he can hold or break, Maren asks what that will do to the captive, and the hero settles the plan. Preserve the personal-story lines and develop that specificity elsewhere. Give guild recruitment a first spoken line and a reason Nessa chooses this party; let her refer to the rescue only if it actually happened.

**Severity:** major.

### 3. Lysa can be released twice, undoing the scene's human consequence

**Where:** `ambush-aftermath`, `offer-mercy` followed by `threaten-lookout`, turns 16–17.

**What:** My failed persuasion produced: “Lysa refuses names but accepts treatment. Before leaving, she warns that the bell ‘remembers who betrayed it.’” The threat choice remained available. I deliberately selected it and succeeded: “Lysa gives the route and Sable's hand-sign, then crawls away when released.” Maren's approval rose and then fell, but Lysa effectively departed twice.

**Why it matters:** Mercy loses its meaning as a commitment when I can immediately interrogate the person the story has just sent away.

**Suggestion:** Make mercy and threats alternative conclusions to the conversation. Once either outcome releases Lysa, remove the other and allow only searching the abandoned satchel or leaving. If she is intended to remain after failed persuasion, explicitly say she stays, and acknowledge the hero breaking their promise when switching to threats.

**Severity:** major.

### 4. Nessa helps at the crossing before she has joined

**Where:** `underroad`, `rope-crossing`, turn 20; outcome displayed on arrival at `root-gate`.

**What:** “One by one the party crosses while Orin anchors the line and Nessa watches the dark shaft.” My party at that moment contained only Orin and Maren. I had not visited Nessa's rescue scene; she first joined at Ashford on turn 36.

**Why it matters:** An unintroduced companion appearing in the action makes the party's shared history unreliable.

**Suggestion:** Make the hero watch the shaft on this route, or use Nessa's version only when she is present. Keep Orin anchoring the rope: it suits him.

**Severity:** major.

### 5. The terminal skips the road expedition's actual opening

**Where:** departure to `q-road-start`, turn 29.

**What:** Departure printed “The party departs for The Toll of Empty Wagons. Returning to town will preserve resolved checks, merchant stock and consequences,” followed by investigation choices. After finishing, `read q-road-start` revealed an opening I had never seen during play: “An abandoned wagon stands with its wheels chocked, not broken. From the tollhouse beyond, a bell repeats three short notes. A driver behind a shutter flashes a hand and vanishes.”

**Why it matters:** The strongest immediate evidence that someone is trapped is absent from the prescribed playtest experience, leaving me to investigate a scene I have barely been shown.

**Suggestion:** On departure, print the destination's opening as well as the departure result. Keep the information about preserved progress in a separate help message. This finding concerns the terminal path tested here; I did not check the browser presentation.

**Severity:** minor.

### 6. The road approach is a rules explanation in place of a location

**Where:** `q-road-approach`, reached through `q-road-start` / `advance`; also `ending` / `report-bell` and civic lodging at `town-briarwatch`.

**What:** The approach opening is “The route ahead offers a careful approach or a direct crossing. Choose how to handle the obstacle; supplies and trained skills can change the risk.” Both the preceding choice and the next careful choice say “Cross under the causeway and reach the drivers.” The transition therefore adds a screen without showing a new physical situation. Other result text says “Town stock refreshed after new progress, not after opening a menu” and “No XP or stock refresh is granted.”

**Why it matters:** Generic instructions and economy explanations interrupt the story and make a promising rescue feel like steps through a checklist.

**Suggestion:** Describe the underside of the causeway, the sentries above and the route to the yard, then offer the crossing choice once. Put odds, resource rules and persistence guarantees in clearly separate help or status text. Retain those useful guarantees without making them the narration.

**Severity:** minor.

### 7. The opening objective sends me after someone who is already home

**Where:** `briarwatch-square`, objective and `visit-tovin`.

**What:** The goal is “Learn why the ward-bell fell silent, then follow the missing bell-keeper into the Briarwood.” On the same screen, Tovin's visit description says he survived and returned from the wood; I then spoke to him in the infirmary.

**Why it matters:** The opening gives conflicting answers about whom I am trying to find.

**Suggestion:** “Learn why the ward-bell fell silent, then follow Tovin's trail into the Briarwood.”

**Severity:** minor.

## Tone, comedy and limits

The rain, shuttered windows, invisible bell rope in Tovin's hands and root-grown gate fit the grounded fantasy tone. No forced quip or joke at a wounded person's expense appeared on this route. That restraint should survive revision. There was too little actual conversation to judge the bible's character-based humour, Orin and Maren's sibling-like bickering, or Nessa's fast voice; add situations that let them speak, rather than inserting jokes to meet a quota.

The failed checks generally kept the story moving. The danger is less repeated failure than repeated thin screens: the route gets from an intriguing image to its resolution before people have much chance to react. The drivers-versus-escort decision offers a useful immediate-need-versus-future-safety tradeoff, but I stopped at level 3 and cannot judge its later consequences. Likewise, this run does not establish later companion continuity, long-term balance, or whether the Gloam and the wider plot pay off. No broad automated test suite was run: this was a story playtest, and only this review was added to the tracked files.

## Three things to keep

1. **Failure reveals something human instead of closing the story.** Failing to calm Tovin still leaves his scratched shrine arch; failing the road investigation still reveals that the prisoners live. Both invite continued attention.
2. **Preparation can replace violence.** The cult seal opens the gate, the ledger defeats Sable peacefully, and duplicate receipts free the drivers. These were usable resolutions in this run, not promises of an alternative that still forces a fight.
3. **The companions' personal motives are concrete.** Orin's abandoned civilians and Maren's missing keepers and their families give each a distinct reason to travel. Build conversations around those lines.
